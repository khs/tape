## What's on this dashboard

Three sets of forward-looking series, each from the institution that
publishes the projection — not a Tape-generated forecast.

| Set | Projection horizon | Vintage cadence |
|---|---|---|
| **CBO budget baseline** | 10 years | Twice yearly (Jan + Aug) |
| **SSA Trustees Report** | 75 years | Annual (May/June) |
| **Energy futures curves** | ~18 months | Every pipeline refresh |

In every chart, the solid line is historical actuals and the dashed
line is the projection. The renderer extends the x-axis automatically
to fit the projection's last point.

## How to read a "baseline" projection

CBO and SSA baselines are mechanically defined: they assume current
law is followed exactly, with no extension of expiring provisions and
no new legislation. That's a useful counterfactual but it's not a
realistic forecast — Congress routinely extends expiring tax
provisions, modifies spending caps, and passes new programs. Realized
outcomes usually diverge from the baseline, typically in the direction
of higher outlays and bigger deficits.

The CBO charts here show **only the baseline**. CBO also publishes
alternative scenarios that incorporate likely policy changes — those
aren't included on Tape (yet); the agency itself notes that the
alternative scenarios involve more judgment.

## How to read a "Trustees Report" projection

The SSA Office of the Chief Actuary publishes three scenarios each
year — low-cost (I), intermediate (II), and high-cost (III) — based
on alternative assumptions about fertility, mortality, immigration,
labor force participation, and productivity growth. The intermediate
scenario is presented as the actuary's best estimate, and is what
every external commentator cites by default. That's what's plotted
here as the dashed extension.

The trustees themselves describe the long-range projections as "an
indication of the program's likely future direction under a fairly
wide range of conditions" — they aren't precise predictions, and the
75-year horizon means small assumption changes compound to big
differences at the end. The famous "trust fund depletion year" has
moved back and forth by several years across successive reports.

## How to read a futures curve

A futures curve isn't a forecast in the same sense as a CBO baseline
or an SSA projection. It's the actual price at which market
participants are willing to buy and sell contracts that deliver the
commodity in each future month. So it represents:

- **A consensus of market participants' expected prices**, weighted
  by who is willing to put money behind each view.
- **A risk-adjusted view** — buyers value certainty, sellers value
  storage costs and convenience yield, so the curve incorporates
  more than pure expectation.
- **An option to act**: anyone who disagrees with the curve can
  trade against it, which is why persistent curve mispricings tend
  to be eaten.

For oil specifically, the curve is the cleanest public price-forecast
available. It's not always right (1- to 4-month errors average $8-10
per barrel in WTI backtests), but it's the best price-discovery
mechanism we have.

## Customizing this dashboard

Every source on this dashboard is in the composer library. The
"Customize" link at the top opens this dashboard pre-loaded in the
composer so you can swap in CBO scenarios against the SSA cost
trajectory, overlay current futures curves against the most recent
EIA outlook, or build your own scenario stack.

The vintage picker (Phase 3 of the Forecast UI) is not yet shipped —
when it lands, you'll be able to compare "what CBO thought in 2023"
vs "what CBO thinks now" on the same chart. The vintage history is
already being captured by the pipelines, so when the picker ships
it'll pick up the back-data automatically.
