## Why the headline rate isn't enough

The U3 unemployment rate is the most-quoted number in economic news,
and it's also one of the least-informative single labor-market gauges.
At 4.5% U3, the labor market could be:

- **Tight + healthy**: lots of openings, few claims, high quits,
  rising real wages. Pre-COVID 2019.
- **Tight + breakdown about to start**: openings collapsing, claims
  rising, but the official rate hasn't caught up yet because the
  newly-displaced haven't filed for benefits. Early 2008.
- **Soft + masked**: participation falling, people quitting looking
  faster than they're losing jobs. 2014.

The same 4.5% means three completely different things in those
scenarios. The other panels here — claims, JOLTS, participation —
disambiguate.

## Reading claims data

Initial UI claims are released weekly, two weeks after the reference
week. That's the highest-frequency labor data the US government
publishes. Two things to know:

- **The 4-week moving average is what matters.** Weekly numbers
  bounce around due to seasonal factors that the seasonal adjustment
  doesn't perfectly catch (especially around holidays, school
  schedules, hurricanes). The 4-week MA cuts through the noise.
- **Continuing claims lag.** Continuing claims = currently receiving
  benefits, which requires filing initially weeks ago + continuing
  to qualify. Best for tracking whether the unemployed are getting
  reabsorbed — rising continuing claims with flat initial claims
  means the labor market is freezing up (people who lose jobs can't
  find new ones).

In recessions, initial claims typically spike *before* the headline
unemployment rate ticks up — by 3-6 weeks. So claims are a true
leading indicator.

## JOLTS as the bargaining-power lens

The Job Openings + Labor Turnover Survey came out of academic
labor economics in the 1990s and got mainstreamed during the
2010s. Two key series:

- **Job openings** — total unfilled positions. Demand for labor.
  Pre-COVID baseline ~7M; peaked at 12M in early 2022 during the
  Great Resignation; back to ~8M by 2024.
- **Quits rate** — voluntary departures as % of employed. When
  workers feel confident they can find another job, quits rise.
  The "Great Resignation" was really a Great Quitting Period —
  the quits rate touched 3% in 2022 vs a long-run average ~2%.

The ratio of openings to unemployed is the cleanest tightness
gauge: 2+ means employers are bidding for scarce workers; below
1 means workers are bidding for scarce jobs. Late-2022 hit a
record 2.0; 2024 normalized to 1.1.

## Participation — the demographic and the cyclical

Labor force participation has two moving parts that pull in
opposite directions:

1. **Demographic** — Baby Boomers aged into retirement starting
   2008; overall participation has been falling since then for
   purely demographic reasons unrelated to labor-market health.
2. **Cyclical** — Working-age people drop out during recessions
   (discouraged, going back to school, taking care of kids) and
   come back during expansions.

Prime-age (25-54) participation filters out the demographic
component. It's the cleanest "are working-age people working"
metric, and it's nearly back to its 2007 peak for the first time
since the GFC — a quietly underrated post-COVID success story.

## State-level variation

Top-10 state unemployment and payrolls let you see which parts
of the country are running hot or cold. A few patterns that
recur:

- **DC + federal-employment states** (VA, MD, DC) consistently run
  ~1-2 percentage points below national unemployment. Federal-
  worker job security plus the contractor base.
- **Energy states** (TX, ND, WY, OK) track the commodity cycle —
  unemployment spikes during oil-price crashes (2015-16, 2020),
  drops below national during oil booms.
- **Manufacturing-heavy Midwest** (OH, IN, MI, WI) tracks the
  goods cycle and industrial production. Slower secular trend
  away from manufacturing employment masks the cyclical pattern.

## Customizing for your labor thesis

A few common composer moves:

- **Wage growth net of inflation:** divide avg_hourly_earnings by
  us_cpi → real-wage trend.
- **Beveridge curve:** plot unemployment vs job openings as a
  scatter (via composer's bivariate mode) to see how the
  efficiency of job-matching has shifted.
- **Sector-specific employment:** the BLS series IDs for sector
  employment are in the source library; swap in
  manufacturing/retail/healthcare etc.
