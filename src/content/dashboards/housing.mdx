## Five forces in the US housing cycle

Most pieces about "the housing market" cover one or two of these
and infer the rest. The dashboard separates them out:

1. **Rates** — the largest single variable in monthly affordability.
   30-year fixed mortgage rates dictate how much house a given
   income can buy.
2. **Supply pipeline** — what's being built. Building permits lead
   housing starts lead completions. Constrained supply in coastal
   metros has structurally elevated prices; permissive supply in
   sunbelt metros has kept growth absorbing demand.
3. **Existing-home turnover** — the lock-in effect. Homeowners
   with mortgages locked in at sub-4% pre-2022 don't sell unless
   forced, even when prices rise. Existing-home transaction volume
   has been depressed since rates spiked.
4. **Vacancy + months supply** — slack in the system. Low slack
   means seller's market and rapid price appreciation; high slack
   means buyer's market and prices grind.
5. **Demand drivers** — income growth, household formation,
   immigration, in-migration to specific metros.

The first two are visible directly in the dashboard charts; the
others show up indirectly via prices.

## Reading Case-Shiller

The 20-city Case-Shiller index is the gold-standard US home-price
benchmark because it uses **repeat sales** — tracking the same
property across two transactions, holding the property constant.
Cleaner than median-sale price (which moves with the mix of
properties that happen to be selling that month) and cleaner than
average sale (which mix-shift is even worse).

Two caveats:

- **Geographic concentration.** It's a 20-city composite, weighted
  toward large coastal metros. The national index gets less
  attention but is broader.
- **Slow.** Each data point reflects the average of three months
  of sales lagged about two months — so the November 2024 release
  is really September-October-November 2024 weighted. The series
  moves slowly compared to listings data.

## The lock-in effect explained

When 30-year mortgage rates went from sub-3% in 2021 to 7%+ in
2023, every homeowner with a sub-3% loan effectively saw their
monthly housing cost double if they moved (assuming a similarly-
priced replacement home at the new rate). That created a strong
incentive to *not* move:

- Existing-home transactions dropped ~30% nationally in 2023.
- Total mortgage originations dropped harder — refinance volume
  collapsed first, purchase volume followed.
- The supply pipeline (new construction) became the only
  meaningful source of incremental for-sale inventory.

This is why "lower mortgage rates" is a structural unlock for
housing transaction volume in a way it usually isn't. Until rates
come back down toward where existing mortgages are locked in,
transaction volume stays depressed.

## DC-metro divergence

DC-area housing runs differently from the national average for
two structural reasons:

1. **Income stability.** Federal employment is ~12% of the metro
   workforce (vs ~2% nationally), and federal pay doesn't
   cyclically shed during recessions. Housing demand stays
   firmer through downturns, prices don't fall as far.
2. **Supply constraint.** Especially in inner NoVa (Arlington,
   Alexandria, Falls Church) and inner MD (Bethesda, Silver
   Spring), local zoning + topography limit new construction.
   Prices clear higher.

The DC Case-Shiller-vs-US chart shows the trajectory: DC ran with
the national index through the GFC drop, then started pulling
ahead in 2012-13, widened the gap further through the
federal-spending recovery and tech-policy buildout of the 2010s,
and now sits ~30% above the national index in absolute terms.

## Customizing for housing analysis

Common composer moves:

- **Real home prices**: divide Case-Shiller by us_cpi to net out
  inflation; useful for "are houses actually getting more
  expensive in real terms" questions.
- **Affordability index**: combine mortgage_30y + median_home_price
  + a wage series to get a payment-to-income proxy.
- **Inventory months / new construction**: ratio of months_supply
  to building_permits as a "is supply catching up" signal.
