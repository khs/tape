## Why so many flavors of "inflation"

The cleanest answer to "what's inflation right now?" is *which*
inflation:

| Measure | Who calls this their main one | Why it's different |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline CPI** | Cost-of-living adjustments (Social Security, contracts) | Most familiar; volatile because food + energy are in |
| **Core CPI** | Most journalism, common parlance | Strips out food + energy; what people mean when they say "inflation is sticky" |
| **Headline PCE** | Fed's nominal 2% target | Different basket weight; methodology lets it move as consumers substitute |
| **Core PCE** | Fed's effective real target | Fed funds rate decisions key on this |

The four series move together over years but diverge over months
and quarters. Headline CPI is most prone to one-off spikes
(gas-price shock, food-supply disruption); core PCE is the smoothest.
At any given moment one of them is almost certainly running above
2% while another is running below, picking the "right" one is a
framing choice as much as a measurement one.

## Where the components matter

The CPI-component charts split the headline number into the pieces
that move differently:

- **Food + energy** are stripped out of "core" because they bounce
  on shocks unrelated to monetary policy. OPEC supply decisions,
  bird flu, weather, none of which the Fed can iterate on.
- **Shelter** is roughly a third of headline CPI by weight and the
  single largest contributor to core. BLS measures it via Owners'
  Equivalent Rent plus actual rents, weighted by tenure. Because
  most leases roll over at 12 months, shelter CPI lags
  asking-rent indices (Zillow, Apartment List) by about a year.
  That's why core stayed elevated through 2023 even after market
  rents had topped out in mid-2022.
- **Medical** has run consistently above headline for decades.
  The methodology BLS uses to capture health-insurance prices has
  been revised twice in the last fifteen years; each revision
  meaningfully changed the medical-CPI trajectory.

## Reading the breakeven curves

Breakeven inflation is the bond market's forecast, embedded in
prices: the spread between a nominal Treasury yield and the
TIPS yield at the same maturity gives the average annual CPI
implied by the two. If 10-year nominals are at 4.5% and 10-year
TIPS at 2.0%, the market is pricing 2.5% average inflation over
the next decade.

Two important caveats:

1. **Breakevens include a risk premium.** Holding TIPS shifts
   inflation risk from the holder to the Treasury, which costs
   the Treasury extra yield. So breakevens systematically run
   slightly above the "pure" expected-inflation number, by
   maybe 30-50 bps.
2. **TIPS markets can stress.** During 2008 and 2020, TIPS
   liquidity dropped sharply and breakevens cratered, reflecting
   market microstructure, not actual inflation expectations.
   Filter out those windows when reading the long-run trend.

The breakeven curves are nonetheless the cleanest market-derived
view of what investors think average inflation will run, and they
move in advance of CPI data more often than they lag.

## Customizing for your inflation thesis

Open the composer to swap in your own series. Common moves:

- **Goods vs services CPI:** drill into BLS's published splits to
  see whether the persistent inflation is in services (sticky,
  domestic-economy-driven) or goods (more transitory, global-
  supply-chain-driven).
- **Compare to the 1970s:** add fred/us_cpi at a 50y window and
  see how today's trajectory compares to the Volcker era.
- **Wage growth vs CPI:** divide fred/avg_hourly_earnings by
  fred/us_cpi to track real wage growth.
