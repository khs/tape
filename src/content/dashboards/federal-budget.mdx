## What the federal-budget story looks like

US federal finances split cleanly into four moving parts:

| Part | What it is | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| **Outlays** | What the government spends in a fiscal year | "outlays / GDP" chart |
| **Receipts** | What it collects in taxes | "receipts / GDP" chart |
| **Deficit** | Outlays − receipts. Funded by borrowing. | "deficit / GDP" chart |
| **Debt** | Accumulated past deficits, still outstanding | "debt / GDP" chart |

Receipts have hovered near 17% of GDP for three quarters of a century
— the famous "Hauser's Law" observation. Outlays drift up over time
as entitlement programs mature and the population ages. The gap
between the two lines is the annual deficit; integrated over decades
it's the debt-to-GDP trajectory.

## Why interest crossed defense in 2024

For the first time since World War II, federal net interest payments
exceeded the defense budget in 2024 — visible in the spending-by-
function chart. Two forces drove the crossover:

1. **Stock effect.** Debt-to-GDP roughly doubled between 2007 and
   2024 (from ~62% to ~120%) as the GFC + COVID emergency spending
   ratcheted the stock up.
2. **Flow effect.** The Fed's 2022–23 tightening pushed the average
   coupon rate on new Treasury issuance from under 2% to over 4.5%.
   As old low-rate debt rolls off and gets reissued at higher rates,
   the effective rate on the whole stock climbs.

The combination is mechanical: even with the deficit held constant,
higher rates × bigger stock = higher annual interest. The crossover
isn't a one-time event — at current rates and a debt trajectory
that's still expanding faster than GDP, interest as a share of the
budget keeps climbing.

## Reading the geography panel

The "where the money lands" charts use **prime-recipient location**
— where the contractor or grantee has its primary office, not where
the work is performed. This matters for two reasons:

- **VA + MD + DC capture the contracting economy.** The DC-metro
  prime contractors (Northrop, Lockheed, Booz Allen, MITRE) book
  contract dollars to their HQ jurisdictions, even when the work
  is done in Huntsville, San Diego, or Colorado Springs. That's the
  right metric for "what is the DC region's federal economy" but
  the wrong one for "what does each state's resident economy receive
  in federal money."
- **Welfare + transfers are different.** Social Security, Medicare,
  veterans' benefits etc. flow as direct payments to residents at
  their address, not the contract-prime address. Those don't show
  up disproportionately in the contracting-corridor states.

For a per-resident view of federal money, the demographic-context
heatmaps below help — places with high poverty + foreign-born share
+ lower educational attainment receive more per-capita
transfer-payment outlay even if their state's contract-dollar count
is small.

## Customizing this dashboard

Every source on this dashboard is in the composer library. The
"Customize" link at the top opens this dashboard pre-loaded in the
composer so you can swap charts in or out, add your own series,
or save your version under a unique URL.

For the share-of-GDP angle on any specific spending line, use the
composer's derived-source feature: add the raw dollars series, then
divide by `fred/gdp_nominal` to get the same level-on-GDP that the
fiscal headline charts use.
