## District snapshot (ACS 5-year, 2022 vintage)

| Measure | Value | National comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Population | **792,500** | Median CD ~770,000 (sized equal at each decennial) |
| Median household income | **$126,200** | US national median ~$74,500 (1.7× national) |
| Median age | 37.1 | National median ~38.9 |
| Residents in poverty | 58,700 (**7.4%**) | US national rate ~11.5% |
| Adults with bachelor's degree (just) | 175,600 | Substantial additional population has graduate degrees on top |
| **Foreign-born share** | **27.2%** | National foreign-born share ~13.9% — VA-08 nearly double |
| Veteran population (civilian) | 49,400 (6.2%) | National veteran share ~6.0% — at the national rate despite DoD-adjacency |
| Owner-occupied households | 171,100 (51.6%) | National homeownership ~65% — VA-08 is a renter-heavy district |
| Median home value | **$695,100** | US median ~$280,000 (2.5× national) |
| Median gross rent | **$2,072/mo** | US median ~$1,300/mo (1.6× national) |
| Households with broadband | 311,500 (94.0%) | National rate ~88% |

The five-year nominal-income trajectory ($104k → $126k = +21% from 2017
to 2022) tracks ~2 percentage points above national CPI inflation over
the same window — a modest real-income gain on top of inflation. The
trajectory chart below shows VA-08 sitting in the middle of the
DC-metro pack; VA-10 (Wexton — Loudoun-and-outer-Fairfax) is the
metro's richest CD.

## How these numbers are computed

Most figures above are aggregated from tract-level ACS data using
Census block-assignment files and the 2010↔2020 block correspondence
tables. So "VA-08 median household income, 2014" is computed on
*current* 118th-Congress VA-08 geography, not the geography that was
in effect during the 113th Congress when the original ACS 2014
measurement was made.

For median household income specifically, the computation goes one
step further: we sum the 16 household-income bins from the B19001
distribution table across all tracts assigned to current VA-08, then
compute the weighted median of the aggregated distribution. This is
a true CD-level median rather than the population-weighted-mean-of-
tract-medians approximation other CD-aggregation efforts use.

The medians for age, home value, and rent are currently
contemporaneous-CD-level (i.e. the geography that was in effect each
vintage). Recomputing them via distribution tables is a future
addition — same technique as for income.

**Coverage caveat.** The tract-level stable-geo aggregation currently
runs 2017–2022; the Census API's tract-level endpoints for pre-2017
ACS5 vintages haven't returned data for our fetcher, an issue to
investigate. The contemporaneous-CD-level medians (age, home value,
rent) extend back to ACS 2010 as a partial fallback.

## What "federal spending in VA-08" actually means

The USAspending.gov data we use here attributes dollars by **prime
recipient location** — where the contractor's primary office is, not
where the work is performed. For VA-08, this is a feature, not a bug:
the district's economic identity is built around being the place those
prime contractors are headquartered. A Crystal City defense firm
hiring engineers in Huntsville still books the contract dollars to
VA-08, and that's the right metric for "how does federal money
interact with this place economically."

It does mean the figures aren't directly comparable to "federal
spending per capita on residents" in the welfare-and-transfers sense.
Aggregated, our 50-state sum captures about 73% of total federal
outlays — the missing 27% is debt service, some classified spending,
and outlays without a clean recipient-state attribution.

## Three regimes in district federal spending

1. **Pre-2011 growth.** Steady increase through the late-Bush + early-
   Obama buildup — DoD spending, the Stimulus, post-9/11 contractor
   expansion.
2. **2011–2015 flat-and-decline.** The Budget Control Act of 2011
   imposed sequestration on FY2013+. VA-08 felt this sharply because
   so much of the local economy is on prime contracts. Federal
   spending in the district basically didn't grow for half a decade.
3. **Post-2020 step up.** COVID emergency appropriations, then the
   IRA/CHIPS/IIJA tranche of Biden-era industrial policy. Spending
   levels are now well above the 2009 stimulus peak.

VA-11 (Loudoun + outer Fairfax — Gerry Connolly's seat) has grown
faster than VA-08 over the same period as data-center construction
and contractor satellite offices spread west along the Dulles
corridor. VA-08's spending base is more mature; VA-11's is the
marginal-growth district for the DC contracting economy.

## Labor market resilience

DC-metro unemployment runs about a percentage point below the US
headline rate in almost every period. The spread widens during
downturns:

- **2008–2010.** US unemployment peaked at 10.0%; the DC-metro peak
  was 7.8%. A 2.2-point gap.
- **2020 COVID.** US peaked at 14.7%; DC-metro at 9.4%. A 5.3-point
  gap, the widest in modern history.

This isn't because the metro is recession-proof — it's because federal
employment is roughly 12% of the regional workforce (vs ~2% nationally),
and the federal payroll doesn't cyclically shed workers the way
manufacturing or hospitality do. Federal-contractor employment is
more cyclical but still less so than private-sector averages, because
most prime contracts have multi-year obligations.

Within VA-08 specifically, Arlington County consistently posts the
lowest unemployment rate of the four jurisdictions, despite being the
most populous. Falls Church City (~15,000 residents) shows the most
volatility because the small base makes the rate noisy.

## Housing as the constraint

DC-metro home prices outpaced the national index from roughly 2012 to
2017 — the federal-spending recovery decade — and then matched the
national 2020–22 housing surge from an elevated starting point. The
listing-price chart shows the resulting level: median list around
$550k as of mid-decade, well above the national ~$430k.

This is the binding constraint on the district as a place to live.
The same federal-economy advantage that explains low unemployment,
high household incomes, and high educational attainment also makes
the local cost-of-living anchor near the top of US metros — pricing
out the people who staff the offices that get this money in the first
place.

## Why this dashboard

Most "congressional district" data is hard to compose — published as
PDF maps, locked behind interactive web tools, or aggregated only to
the state level. VA-08 in particular is a useful case study because:

1. The federal-money story is unusually concentrated and visible
2. Demographic + economic outliers (income, education) make it a
   stress-test of national-vs-local divergence
3. Combined with neighboring districts (VA-11, MD-05, MD-08, DC) it
   tells a clean DC-metro federal-economy story you can't get from
   state-level data alone

All underlying sources are in the composer library if you want to
swap in different districts or build a different angle. The
"Customize this dashboard" link at top opens this one in the composer
pre-loaded.
