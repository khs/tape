## What "recession monitor" can and can't do

No single indicator predicts US recessions reliably enough to bet
on. The 10y-2y curve is the highest-batting-average single signal
(every modern US recession was preceded by inversion), but it's
had false positives (1966) and its lead times vary from 8 to 22
months. The current 2022-onward episode — no NBER-dated recession
yet, despite the curve inverting in mid-2022 and the Sahm rule
triggering in mid-2024 — is the longest lead-without-recession on
record.

What the dashboard *can* do is show the convergence — when many
historically-reliable indicators flip the same direction, the case
strengthens. Each panel here ranks indicators by approximate
batting average × lead time:

1. **Curve** — best single signal, ~18 months lead. Inverted hard
   2022-24.
2. **Bank tightening (SLOOS)** — strong leading indicator, 6-9
   months ahead. Tightened sharply 2022-23.
3. **Initial claims** — leading by 3-6 weeks. Has stayed
   surprisingly low.
4. **Real economy** — coincident or lagging. By the time these
   confirm, the recession is already underway.

## What the indicators got right (and wrong) recently

**2022-25 episode summary:**

| Indicator | Signal | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Yield curve | Inverted hard, NY Fed model peaked at 70%+ | False positive (so far) |
| SLOOS C&I | Tightened to 2008-comparable | No 2008-style credit crunch |
| Initial claims | Briefly elevated then resumed declining | No spike |

This is roughly the historical pattern for a "soft landing"
sequence — early-cycle stress signals that don't propagate to
labor + real economy. The closest historical comp is 1994-95:
Fed hiked aggressively, curve flattened, growth wobbled, but
unemployment stayed low and the cycle continued.

The remaining question is whether the lag is just longer than
average this time (with recession still pending) or whether
something structural about the post-COVID economy weakened the
predictive power of these indicators. The dashboard lets you
track the answer as it develops.

## Why claims and not the unemployment rate

The headline unemployment rate (U3) is a lagging indicator —
typically doesn't turn up until the recession has already started.
Initial claims lead by weeks; sustained increases above ~400k
in the 4-week moving average have historically preceded recessions.

The 2022-25 surprise is that claims stayed in the 200s through
the curve inversion, the SLOOS tightening, and the regional
banking stress of March 2023. Either the labor market got
structurally tighter (less worker turnover = fewer cyclical
layoffs) or labor hoarding by post-COVID-employer-trauma
managers has kept the cycle from showing up where it usually does.

## The Sahm rule, explained

Claudia Sahm published the rule in 2019 to give a fast,
unambiguous, single-number summary of when the labor market has
turned a recession corner. The construction:

1. Take the unemployment rate (U3, monthly).
2. Compute its 3-month moving average.
3. Subtract the trailing 12-month minimum of that MA.
4. The result is the Sahm-rule reading. When it crosses +0.5
   percentage points, the rule "fires."

Why those specific parameters? The 3-month MA smooths through
noisy single-month moves; the 12-month look-back catches the
turn-of-cycle moment (when the lowest UR is still recent enough
to be the cycle low); the 0.5pp threshold was calibrated against
US recessions back to 1959 and triggers cleanly before every
NBER-dated downturn while almost never false-firing.

**Historical track record:** Every US recession since 1970 was
preceded by a Sahm-rule trigger, usually 2-5 months ahead of the
NBER's eventual peak date. No false positives in that window. The
1969-70 recession was the last episode where Sahm would have not
triggered cleanly (the rule was designed before that data, so
strictly that's a forecast not an in-sample fit).

**The 2024 trigger that broke the streak.** In July 2024 the Sahm
rule crossed 0.5pp for the first time since 2020. No recession
followed in the subsequent year. Claudia Sahm herself argued that
the trigger was being driven by unusual post-COVID labor-supply
dynamics — rising prime-age participation, fast immigration
catch-up, voluntary job-switching — rather than the cyclical
layoff signal the rule was designed to detect. The unemployment
rate rising while initial claims stayed low + JOLTS quits rising
is consistent with a "more people looking for jobs" story
rather than the "more people losing jobs" story Sahm calibrated
against.

That doesn't kill the rule — it surfaces an interpretation
challenge. Watching the SAHMREALTIME series (which uses the
unemployment-rate data available at each publication date) is
the honest version: it answers "would this have signaled in
real time" rather than "would this have signaled with the
benefit of revisions."

The chart's 30-year default window shows the 1990, 2001, 2008,
2020, and 2024 triggers in sequence. Drag the window pill to 50y
to see the full back-history including the 1973, 1980, and 1981
episodes.

## Why the real fed funds rate matters here

The Fed's policy stance can only be evaluated against inflation.
A 5% nominal funds rate is loose policy when inflation is 8% and
tight policy when inflation is 2%. The real-fed-funds chart
subtracts realized CPI YoY from the nominal rate to give the
inflation-adjusted policy stance.

A few patterns worth knowing:

- **Every modern recession** was preceded by real fed funds at or
  above +3%. The Volcker era ran much higher (+8%+).
- **Negative real rates** (2020-22) are unambiguously stimulative.
  Borrowers were paying back less in purchasing-power terms than
  they borrowed.
- **The slope matters as much as the level.** A 0% real rate
  reached by cutting from -3% is dovish; a 0% real rate reached
  by hiking from -5% (the 2022-23 path) is the equivalent of a
  500bp tightening.

The "subtract REALIZED CPI" framing here differs from the
"subtract EXPECTED inflation" framing other publications use
(typically subtracting the 10-year breakeven inflation). Both are
defensible. Realized tells you the squeeze borrowers feel right
now; expected tells you the squeeze the bond market thinks is
coming. For recession-watching, realized is the more relevant
quantity — recessions hit when current debt servicing gets
expensive, not when forward expectations move.

## Customizing your recession framework

Common composer moves:

- **Conference Board LEI**: composite of 10 leading indicators
  including some of these. The proprietary version isn't on FRED
  but the constituent series mostly are; you can rebuild a rough
  version.
- **Real fed funds vs breakeven inflation**: swap the realized
  CPI series for breakeven_10y in the real-fed-funds chart to
  see the policy stance vs market-implied inflation expectations.
  Tends to lead the realized version by 6-12 months at turning
  points.
- **Real wages**: divide avg_hourly_earnings by us_cpi to track
  consumer purchasing power. Recessions often start with real
  wages turning negative.
